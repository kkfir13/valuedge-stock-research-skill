# See’s Candies: a $25 million lesson in economic goodwill

**Period:** January 1972 acquisition, discussed retrospectively in the 1983 and 1991 Berkshire Hathaway letters. **Evidence:** first-party shareholder reporting; no current stock recommendation.

## Transaction reconstruction

In the 1991 letter Buffett recounts the January 1972 acquisition of control of See’s by Blue Chip Stamps, then affiliated with Berkshire and later merged into it. Sellers sought the equivalent of $40 million for the full interest eventually acquired; about $10 million in excess cash made the operating enterprise's effective asking price around $30 million. Buffett and Charlie Munger resisted paying more than $25 million, and that offer was accepted.

The 1983 letter describes operating tangible assets of roughly $8 million and about $2 million in after-tax earnings when acquired. These figures explain why simple book-value screens initially understated the company's potential economics.

## The business, rather than a numerical superstition

See’s had customer reputation, occasion-driven repeat buying, and a familiar product. Its intangible franchise allowed price increases that were not wholly dependent on heavy physical capital investment. Buffett used the case to explain *economic goodwill*, the present value of earnings beyond a normal return on required tangible assets. It may exceed, or diverge from, recorded accounting goodwill.

That does not mean a strong brand is immune to competition. It means a researcher must test the mechanism that converts consumer preference into prices, volumes, operating cash and incremental returns.

## Decision model: what to test

1. Understand why customers repeatedly choose the product at its price.
2. Estimate recurring cash after the true cost of maintaining distribution, inventory and stores.
3. Ask whether additional revenue requires proportionate new capital or can be supported with existing assets.
4. Compare the negotiated price with conservative operating value, not only book value.
5. Test what inflation, a lost sales channel or changing tastes would do to that value.

## Risk of misreading history

A historical purchase price cannot be applied unadjusted to today's competitors. Buffett's later understanding reflects years of business ownership; we cannot know every private calculation behind the original bid. This case should inform due diligence, not produce a universal “Buffett would buy” score.
