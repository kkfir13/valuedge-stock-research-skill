# Graham, Munger and Buffett: three different intellectual voices

**Primary evidence:** Buffett's GEICO account in the 1995 Berkshire letter, his 1983 economic-goodwill discussion, and source-publisher bibliographic records.

## Graham: disciplined security analysis

Benjamin Graham, who taught Buffett at Columbia, authored *The Intelligent Investor* in 1949. The book is Graham's work, not Buffett's. Buffett's 1995 letter explicitly describes attending Graham's classes and how that association led him to investigate GEICO. The link to his own historical behavior is unusually direct and documented.

Graham's margin-of-safety tradition is relevant to price, balance sheets and financial risk. But claiming Buffett follows a single mechanical “Graham formula” would overlook his later shift in emphasis.

## The influence of experience

Buffett's 1989 letter regrets treating Berkshire textiles as a cheaply priced security despite poor underlying economics. His 1983 account of See's shows why economic goodwill and pricing power can matter more than the book value of tangible assets. Those are direct primary-source records of how his investment emphasis developed.

## Munger: related, not interchangeable

Charlie Munger's multidisciplinary approach is central to *Poor Charlie's Almanack*, edited by Peter D. Kaufman. It contributes an important dimension to Berkshire history, but statements or frameworks from Munger must be attributed to Munger, not combined into a fictional Buffett quote or persona.
